The Durandal occupies a strategic middle ground in the autonomous unmanned systems market: positioned between expensive, bulky modular systems (such as the Pixhawk Cube family on carrier boards) and unbranded, low-reliability marketplace clones.

🧠 Core Concepts

1. The Monolithic High-Reliability Flight Controller Architecture
What is it? A single-chassis flight management unit (FMU) design that integrates the microcontroller, internal sensor suite, I/O header pinouts, and peripheral ports inside a rigid, CNC-machined anodized aluminum housing without requiring a separate, bulky carrier board.
Why do we use it? Modular two-piece flight controllers (like the Pixhawk Cube + carrier board) add physical height, weight, and mechanical complexity that can make installation difficult in tight fixed-wing fuselages. Conversely, cheap open-board clones lack electromagnetic shielding and sensor redundancy. The Durandal delivers enterprise-tier reliability and sensor isolation in a compact footprint.
How does it work? All microcontrollers, internal IMUs, power filtering capacitors, and JST-GH connector sockets are mounted directly to an internal multilayer printed circuit board housed inside the metal enclosure, with machined cutouts for port headers, micro-USB, and the micro-SD card slot.
Where is it used? High-end autonomous fixed-wing aircraft, mapping drones, commercial multirotors, and unmanned ground vehicles (UGVs).
Example: Upgrading an autonomous survey wing from an entry-level plastic flight controller to the Durandal to prevent IMU thermal drift and reduce airframe weight by $\approx 24\,\text{g}$ compared to a Cube setup.
Prerequisites: Basic familiarity with Pixhawk hardware architecture and Mission Planner GCS software.
2. The STM32 H7 Processing Core ($480\,\text{MHz}$)
What is it? An ARM Cortex-M7 32-bit high-performance microcontroller (specifically the STM32H743IIK6) operating at a core clock frequency of $480\,\text{MHz}$ with $2\,\text{MB}$ flash memory and $1\,\text{MB}$ RAM.
Why do we use it? Modern autonomous flight code executes computationally intensive real-time algorithms: 24-state Extended Kalman Filtering (EKF3), high-rate vibration notch filtering (FFT-based dynamic notch), real-time obstacle avoidance, terrain following, and multi-sensor fusion. Legacy F4 microcontrollers ($168\,\text{MHz}$) frequently hit CPU saturation limits under these workloads.
How does it work? The H7 core runs the NuttX Real-Time Operating System (RTOS) and ArduPilot thread scheduler, executing primary attitude estimation loops and rate controllers at up to $400\,\text{Hz} - 1000\,\text{Hz}$ without processor throttling.
Where is it used? Modern Pixhawk FMUv5X, FMUv6, and high-end enterprise autopilots.
Relationship to other concepts: In the Pixhawk Cube family, the H7 processor is available only on the flagship **Cube Orange**, whereas the entry-level Cube Black relies on the older STM32 F4 ($168\,\text{MHz}$). The Durandal matches Cube Orange compute power at a lower cost.
3. Multi-Sensor Redundancy & Independent Bus Isolation
What is it? The integration of multiple, physically separate Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs), barometric pressure altimeters, and magnetometers connected to the primary MCU across independent serial communication buses.
Why do we use it? Sensor failures in autonomous aviation are fatal if single-point failure modes exist. If an accelerometer saturates from high-frequency motor vibration or an I2C compass line locks up, ArduPilot's EKF can detect the divergence, flag the failing sensor, and switch state estimation seamlessly to the healthy secondary sensor mid-flight.
How does it work? The Durandal pairs an internally vibration-isolated primary IMU (ICM-20689 / BMI088) with an independent secondary IMU (ICM-20602 / ICM-42688) routed over separate SPI buses. Dual high-precision barometers (MS5611) sample atmospheric pressure simultaneously.
Where is it used? All flight-critical autonomous systems requiring fail-operational flight logic.
4. Current-Shunt & Voltage-Divider Analog Telemetry (PM02)
What is it? The hardware power management module that steps down raw LiPo voltage ($2\text{S} - 12\text{S}$) to a stable $+5.3\,\text{V}$ logic supply while outputting two scaled analog voltage signals proportional to battery voltage and current draw.
Why do we use it? The flight controller's internal Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) pins cannot tolerate voltages above $3.3\,\text{V}$. The PM02 scales battery voltage down through a precision resistor divider and converts high motor current into micro-volt drops across a low-resistance shunt resistor.
How does it work? In Mission Planner, the user configures two mathematical multiplier constants: the **Voltage Divider** ($18.182$) and the **Amperes Per Volt** ($36.364\,\text{A/V}$). The autopilot multiplies raw ADC readings by these values to calculate instantaneous pack voltage ($V$) and cumulative milliamp-hour consumption ($\text{mAh}$) in real time.
Where is it used? Wired directly inline between the main flight battery connector (XT60) and the vehicle's electronic speed controllers.

The Servo Rail Power Isolation Rule:
Like all Pixhawk enterprise boards, the center positive rail ($+5\,\text{V}$) along the MAIN and AUX servo header is completely isolated from the internal logic bus. Supplying power via USB or plugging in the PM02 Power Module powers the internal MCU, internal IMUs, and the RC IN receiver pins, but outputs zero voltage to the servo pins. Servos will remain limp and completely unpowered until an external $5.0\,\text{V} - 6.0\,\text{V}$ BEC (from an ESC or dedicated UBEC) is connected directly to the output rail.

🛠️ Practical Implementation: Step-by-Step Bench Commissioning

Painless360 demonstrates an end-to-end workbench setup protocol to validate all internal silicon, sensors, and power calibration before installing the hardware inside an airframe:

Step 1: Environmental Preparation & Clean Mission Planner Installation

  1. Update Ground Station Software: Modern STM32 H7 processors require current Windows USB Virtual COM Port (VCP) drivers and bootloader communication profiles. Uninstall any outdated Mission Planner installations and download the latest stable release from ardupilot.org.
  2. Component Interconnect: Connect the external GPS/compass puck into the dedicated GPS port. Connect the 6-pin Molex Clik-Mate harness from the PM02 Power Module into POWER 1.
  3. Connect USB Directly: Plug a high-quality micro-USB cable directly into a native motherboard USB port on your PC (avoiding unpowered external USB hubs).

Step 2: Automated Firmware Flashing (The Disconnected Rule)

  1. Launch Mission Planner. Do NOT click the green "CONNECT" button in the top-right corner.
  2. Navigate to Initial Setup -> Install Firmware.
  3. Click the target vehicle icon: Plane (for ArduPlane) or Copter (for ArduCopter).
  4. Click Yes to confirm. Mission Planner queries the H7 bootloader directly, automatically detects the Holybro Durandal hardware profile, erases old flash sectors, downloads the official compiled H7 hex binary, and streams it to the MCU.
  5. Once completed, the Durandal's internal piezoelectric buzzer emits a rising confirmation musical chime.
  6. Select the newly enumerated COM port (e.g., COM5 - Durandal) in the top-right dropdown, verify baud rate is set to 115200, and click CONNECT.

Step 3: 6-Axis Accelerometer Calibration

  1. Navigate to Initial Setup -> Mandatory Hardware -> Accelerometer Calibration.
  2. Click Calibrate Accelerometer.
  3. Sequentially position the Durandal through all six geometric planes using the machined rectangular edges of the aluminum casing:
    • Level: Flat on desk, top face pointing upward. Click Done.
    • Left: Resting squarely on its left CNC edge. Click Done.
    • Right: Resting squarely on its right CNC edge. Click Done.
    • Nose Down: Positioned vertically with the front arrow pointing straight down. Click Done.
    • Nose Up: Positioned vertically with the front arrow pointing straight up. Click Done.
    • Back (Inverted): Placed upside down with the bottom resting flat. Click Done.
  4. Critical Technique: Ensure the board is completely motionless before clicking each key; hand vibrations during sampling inject acceleration bias into the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF).

Step 4: Dual Compass (Magnetometer) Live Calibration

  1. Physical Alignment: The Durandal houses an internal magnetometer, while the external GPS puck houses the primary navigation magnetometer. Ensure the forward arrow engraved on the GPS puck is oriented parallel to the forward arrow on top of the Durandal metal case (use double-sided tape temporarily on the bench).
  2. Navigate to Mandatory Hardware -> Compass.
  3. Click Live Calibration.
  4. Pick up both units together and tumble the assembly smoothly through 3D space across all axes (roll, pitch, and yaw) until the graphical sample spheres fill with data points and turn green.
  5. Click OK. Perform a complete power-cycle / reboot of the autopilot to commit the newly calculated hard-iron and soft-iron offset matrices ($X, Y, Z$).

Step 5: PM02 Power Module Configuration

  1. Navigate to Initial Setup -> Optional Hardware -> Battery Monitor.
  2. Set Monitor to: Analog Voltage and Current.
  3. Set Sensor to: 9: Other (or select Holybro PM02 if available in presets).
  4. Set APM Ver to: Pixhawk / Durandal.
  5. Enter the factory-calibrated constants supplied on the Holybro reference card:
    • Voltage Divider: 18.182
    • Amperes Per Volt: 36.364
  6. Connect a charged LiPo battery through the PM02; verify that the Mission Planner HUD instantly reflects the pack's real-time terminal voltage and low-idle current draw.

Progress bar hangs, sample variance exceeds tolerance, or Mission Planner rejects the calibration matrices. Provides stable $+5.0\,\text{V}$ bus power and clean D+/D- differential data lines for STM32 H7 DFU bootloader transfers.

📚 Technical Terminology

Term Technical Definition Why It Matters Practical Workshop Context
STM32 H7 A 32-bit ARM Cortex-M7 microcontroller operating at $480\,\text{MHz}$ with double-precision floating-point hardware. Provides the processing power required to run EKF3 state estimation, dynamic FFT notch filtering, and complex autonomy loops without CPU throttling. The core processor running inside the Holybro Durandal and Pixhawk Cube Orange.
FMUv5X / FMUv6 Pixhawk open-hardware standards defining board pinouts, processor interfaces, and sensor bus routing. Guarantees that ArduPilot and PX4 firmware compile cleanly for third-party commercial flight controllers. The engineering standard to which the Holybro Durandal is designed.
PM02 Power Module An inline hardware regulator providing clean $+5.3\,\text{V}$ logic power along with analog voltage and current monitoring. Protects the flight computer from inductive voltage spikes while monitoring battery capacity ($\text{mAh}$) to trigger safety failsafes. Configured in Mission Planner with a voltage multiplier of $18.182$ and current multiplier of $36.364\,\text{A/V}$.
Voltage Divider A linear circuit composed of two resistors ($R_1, R_2$) that scales high battery voltage down to a safe range ($\le 3.3\,\text{V}$). Allows microcontrollers with $3.3\,\text{V}$ maximum ADC inputs to sample $12\text{S}$ ($50.4\,\text{V}$) battery voltages. Parameter BATT_VOLT_MULT inside Mission Planner's Full Parameter Tree.
Current Shunt An ultra-low resistance, high-precision resistor placed in series with the main battery ground or positive wire. Current flowing through the shunt generates a proportional micro-volt drop ($V = I \cdot R$) that is read by an operational amplifier. Parameter BATT_AMP_PERVLT converts this voltage drop into real-time Amperes.
JST-GH A standardized, vibration-resistant, positive-locking wire connector family featuring a $1.25\,\text{mm}$ pitch. Replaced fragile Molex DF13 connectors across all modern Pixhawk autopilots to prevent in-flight signal disconnects. Used on all Durandal external ports (GPS, TELEM, POWER, CAN).
Micro OSD v2 An external processing board running an analog video overlay chip (MAX7456) driven via serial MAVLink telemetry. Required on enterprise flight controllers (Durandal, Cube) that omit onboard analog video chips. Connected directly to the TELEM 1 port to stream HUD data onto analog FPV goggle screens.

💡 Practical Takeaways: Field Operations & Workshop Rules

  • 🟢 Always Update Mission Planner First: Before plugging the Durandal into your PC for the first time, install the latest stable build of Mission Planner to ensure proper H7 USB drivers and bootloader detection profiles are registered.
  • 🔴 Propellers 100% Removed on the Bench: Never connect a LiPo battery to configure the PM02 power monitor or test motor direction with propellers attached. Uncalibrated ESC signals or failsafe triggers can spin motors to full power instantaneously.
  • 🟡 Plug Into POWER 1: Always seat the 6-pin power module cable firmly into POWER 1. The Durandal supports dual power modules, but primary voltage monitoring defaults to port 1.
  • ⚠️ The Servo Rail Is Dead Without an External BEC: Remember that USB power and the PM02 do not supply voltage to the center positive rail on `MAIN OUT 1–8`. Servos require an external $5.0\,\text{V} - 6.0\,\text{V}$ BEC (from an ESC or dedicated UBEC).
  • 🟢 Use the Machined Metal Edges for Calibration: The rigid, flat, 90-degree CNC aluminum sides of the Durandal provide true geometric datums. Rest the case squarely on each edge during 6-axis accelerometer calibration for accurate horizon offsets.
  • 🟢 Align GPS and FC Arrows Before Compass Calibration: When performing live compass calibration, ensure the forward arrow on the GPS puck is oriented in the exact same heading as the arrow on the Durandal metal case.

❓ Questions & Answers (Self-Assessment)

Q1: How does the Holybro Durandal compare to the Pixhawk Cube family in terms of processing power, weight, and physical form factor?
The Durandal integrates an STM32 H7 processor running at $480\,\text{MHz}$, matching the computational performance of the flagship Cube Orange and outperforming the older STM32 F4 ($168\,\text{MHz}$) in the Cube Black. In terms of weight, the Durandal plus GPS weighs $\approx 101.5\,\text{g}$, saving $\approx 24\,\text{g}$ compared to a Cube setup. Mechanically, it uses a monolithic CNC aluminum casing that integrates all ports onto a single chassis, eliminating the need for an external carrier board.
Q2: Why must Mission Planner be updated to the latest version before flashing firmware onto the Durandal?
The STM32 H7 architecture utilizes modern DFU bootloaders and USB Virtual COM Port (VCP) device IDs. Updating Mission Planner ensures that current Windows USB drivers are registered, allowing the software to automatically detect the Durandal board type and flash official ArduPilot H7 binaries without requiring manual board selection or driver recovery tools.
Q3: Why won't servos move when plugged into the MAIN OUT pins if the Durandal is powered via USB or the PM02 Power Module?
The center positive pin ($+5\text{V}$) of the MAIN and AUX servo rails is electrically isolated from the flight controller's internal logic power bus. This safety design prevents inductive back-EMF voltage spikes and high stall currents caused by servo motors from browning out or rebooting the primary MCU. An external $5.0\,\text{V} - 6.0\,\text{V}$ BEC must be connected directly to the output rail to energize the servos.
Q4: What specific parameter multipliers must be entered in Mission Planner to calibrate the Holybro PM02 Power Module?
In the Battery Monitor configuration screen, the Voltage Divider parameter must be set to 18.182, and the Amperes Per Volt parameter must be set to 36.364. These constants match the hardware resistor network and current-sensing shunt inside the PM02 module.
Q5: How does a builder add On-Screen Display (OSD) telemetry to an analog FPV video feed when using the Durandal?
Because the Durandal lacks an onboard analog MAX7456 OSD generator chip, the builder must install an external MAVLink OSD module (such as the Holybro Micro OSD v2). The module connects to the TELEM 1 or TELEM 2 serial port, taps into the analog camera video input, overlays real-time MAVLink telemetry onto the image, and outputs the combined signal to the video transmitter (VTX).
