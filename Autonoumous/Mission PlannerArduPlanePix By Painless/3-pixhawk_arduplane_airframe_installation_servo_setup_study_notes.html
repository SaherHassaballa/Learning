This tutorial advances from open-bench commissioning to physical integration inside an aircraft airframe (demonstrated on a SonicModell / ZOHD Nano Talon V-tail pusher). It bridges the gap between software calibration and physical aerodynamic actuation, demonstrating that the commissioning protocol remains scale-invariant—whether applied to an 860mm backpack foam flyer or a 4-meter, 25kg commercial survey drone.

🧠 Core Concepts

1. The Two-Stage Control Surface Validation Protocol
What is it? A mandatory dual-verification procedure requiring the builder to test manual pilot stick response in MANUAL mode, followed by physical airframe attitude disturbance testing in FBWA (Fly-By-Wire A) mode.
Why do we use it? Manual stick response proves only that the transmitter can deflect control horns. It does not prove that ArduPlane's internal PID stabilization loops will push surfaces in the correct direction. If the autopilot's corrective feedback is inverted, the aircraft will violently diverge upon engaging stabilization, resulting in an unrecoverable crash within seconds of launch.
How does it work? In MANUAL, the pilot deflects gimbals and observes physical throws. Then, switching to FBWA, the pilot manually rolls and pitches the fuselage by hand. The autopilot's attitude estimation must command control surfaces to oppose hand motion (e.g., rolling the right wing up must force the right aileron upward to push the wing back down toward level).
Where is it used? Every autonomous fixed-wing, VTOL, and elevon aircraft commissioning workflow prior to maiden flight.
Example: When the right wing is lifted by hand, the right aileron must rise. If it drops, it will aerodynamically increase lift on the already high wing, compounding the roll into an immediate ground strike.
Prerequisites: Completed 6-axis accelerometer calibration and baseline radio calibration.
2. ArduPlane Internal Mixing Architecture (SERVOx_FUNCTION)
What is it? An internal software routing matrix that maps raw 4-channel pilot demands (Aileron, Elevator, Throttle, Rudder) to designated physical output pins (Output 1–8) while mathematically combining axes for V-tails, elevons, flaperons, or differential thrust.
Why do we use it? To strictly prevent transmitter-side mixing. The handheld radio must output pure single-axis commands so the autopilot receives uncorrupted demand setpoints. ArduPlane handles all aerodynamic mixing internally.
How does it work? By setting SERVO1_FUNCTION = 4 (Aileron), SERVO2_FUNCTION = 79 (V-Tail Left), and SERVO3_FUNCTION = 80 (V-Tail Right), ArduPlane applies its dynamic mixer algorithms directly before outputting PWM pulses to the servo pins. Surface inversions are handled exclusively by checking the Reverse box in Mission Planner's Servo Output menu.
Where is it used? All fixed-wing and hybrid VTOL platforms governed by ArduPilot.
Cardinal Rule: Never reverse a channel on your transmitter to fix a backwards surface. Reversing the channel on the radio inverts manual stick control relative to autopilot stabilization logic, guaranteeing an instant crash.
3. Dynamic In-Flight Auto-Trim (SERVO_AUTO_TRIM)
What is it? An autonomous background parameter routine in ArduPlane (SERVO_AUTO_TRIM = 1) that samples the integrator effort of the stabilization loops during straight-and-level cruise and permanently writes those offsets into the servo neutral registers (SERVOx_TRIM).
Why do we use it? Trimming a physical fixed-wing aircraft manually using transmitter trim rockers is hazardous, tedious, and alters the transmitter's neutral pulse datum away from $1500\,\mu\text{s}$. SERVO_AUTO_TRIM automates aerodynamic trim in the background without human stick intervention.
How does it work? While cruising in FBWA with sticks centered, ArduPlane monitors how much control deflection the PID loops apply to keep wings level. Every 10 seconds, it saves these offsets into the neutral sub-trims. When the pilot toggles back to MANUAL mode, the aircraft remains trimmed for level flight.
Where is it used? Fixed-wing maiden sorties and post-airframe modification test flights.
4. AHRS Level Cruise Pitch Bias (+3° to +5° Nose-Up Offset)
What is it? The practice of calibrating the artificial horizon (AHRS Level) with the aircraft's nose propped slightly upward ($+3^\circ$ to $+5^\circ$, or up to $+8^\circ$ on small foam wings) relative to a flat workbench.
Why do we use it? Fixed-wing aircraft require a positive wing Angle of Attack (AoA) to generate lift ($L = \frac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_L$). If the autopilot is leveled with the fuselage sitting dead flat on a table, it will command $0^\circ$ pitch in autonomous cruise, causing the aircraft to steadily lose altitude unless the pilot constantly pulls back on the elevator stick.
How does it work? Placing a shim under the nose during Calibrate Level forces the autopilot to recognize that slight nose-up attitude as $0^\circ$ cruise pitch. In flight, the aircraft cruises straight and level at neutral throttle.

🔴
The Servo Rail Power Isolation Boundary:
Connecting the USB cable or flight battery through the Power Module energizes the STM32 microcontroller, internal IMUs, and the RC IN receiver pins. It does not deliver power to the center positive rail (+5V) of MAIN OUT (1–8). Servos will remain limp and unresponsive on the bench until an external 5V BEC (typically the red wire from the ESC's throttle cable on Output 8, or a dedicated UBEC) energizes the output rail. This isolation is intentional: it protects the flight computer from inductive voltage spikes and stall-current brownouts caused by servos.
⚠️
The Power Module Bay Trap (Mini Carrier Board):
On dual-power carrier boards, the Power Module lead must be plugged into POWER 1 (PM1). Plugging into PM2 without specific advanced parameter reassignment will power the board, but Mission Planner's HUD will read $0.0\,\text{V}$ and $0.0\,\text{A}$, disabling low-voltage failsafes.
🟢
The Side-Fuselage USB Service Port:
An autopilot requires routine USB access for tuning and DataFlash log retrieval. Rather than removing wings or unbolting hatches between flights, use an X-Acto hobby knife to cut a clean rectangular service port through the foam side wall directly aligned with the micro-USB connector on the carrier board.

🛠️ Practical Implementation: Airframe Commissioning Sequence

Follow this exact chronological sequence to install the electronics and calibrate surfaces safely:

Phase 1: Mechanical Packaging & Forward Orientation

  1. Verify Autopilot Orientation: Mount the Pixhawk Purple Cube and mini carrier board flat in the center cabin near the vehicle Center of Gravity (CG). Ensure the molded forward arrow points toward the nose of the plane.
  2. Mount the GPS/Compass Puck: Affix the external GPS puck to the top exterior of the fuselage, ensuring its forward arrow is parallel to the Pixhawk. Keep it elevated and separated from high-current motor wiring to avoid electromagnetic interference (EMI).
  3. Route Lower Bay Peripherals: Secure the Power Module to the fuselage side wall. Mount the ESC along the lower centerline for airflow. Mount the receiver and piezo buzzer toward the front/rear compartments.
  4. Cut USB Access Port: Align an X-Acto blade with the micro-USB connector on the mini carrier board and cut a clean service window through the foam fuselage wall.

Phase 2: Wiring Documentation (The Builder's Crib Sheet)

  1. Document Output Header Connections: Before closing hatches, write down the exact physical servo assignments:
    • Output 1: Ailerons (routed via dual-servo Y-harness)
    • Output 2: V-Tail Left Servo (or Elevator on conventional tail)
    • Output 3: V-Tail Right Servo (or Rudder on conventional tail)
    • Output 8: Motor Electronic Speed Controller (ESC)
  2. Connect Receiver to RC IN: Wire the serial receiver lead (S.Bus / CRSF) to the RC IN port on the carrier board.

Phase 3: Mission Planner Servo Output Routing (Props Removed!)

  1. Absolute Safety Rule: Remove Propeller! Completely remove the propeller from the motor shaft before connecting batteries or configuring outputs on the bench.
  2. Connect via USB (No Battery Yet): Plug the USB cable through the service port into your PC. Launch Mission Planner and click CONNECT ($115200\text{ baud}$).
  3. Assign Servo Functions: Navigate to: Setup -> Mandatory Hardware -> Servo Output. Assign functions matching your crib sheet:
    • Output 1: Select Aileron (Value: 4)
    • Output 2: Select VtailLeft (Value: 79) — or Elevator (19)
    • Output 3: Select VtailRight (Value: 80) — or Rudder (21)
    • Output 8: Select Throttle (Value: 70)
  4. Subtrim Re-Center & Radio Recalibration: Power on the radio transmitter. In your transmitter's subtrim menu, fine-tune Channel 1, 2, and 4 so their resting values sit at exactly $1500\,\mu\text{s}$ in Mission Planner's Radio Calibration tab. Re-click Calibrate Radio, sweep all sticks to register endpoints, center controls, and click Click when Done.

Phase 4: Bench Live Testing & Direction Reversal

  1. Connect Flight Battery: Connect the LiPo battery to energize the ESC BEC and power the servo rail. You will hear the Pixhawk's initialization musical chime.
  2. Stage 1: Validate Manual Stick Response (Mode: MANUAL): Switch transmitter to MANUAL mode. Deflect sticks and observe surfaces:
    • Push Roll Right: Right aileron must rise; left aileron must drop.
    • Pull Pitch Back (Climb): Both V-tail surfaces must deflect upward.
    • Push Yaw Right: Left V-tail must deflect upward; right V-tail must deflect downward.
    🔴
    How to Fix Backwards Surfaces:
    If a control surface deflects in reverse, check the Reverse checkbox for that specific output pin in Mission Planner's Servo Output menu (e.g., check Reverse on Output 1 for ailerons or Output 2 for V-tail left). DO NOT reverse the channel in your handheld transmitter!
  3. Stage 2: Validate Autopilot Stabilization (Mode: FBWA): Switch transmitter to FBWA (Fly-By-Wire A) mode. Pick up the aircraft and physically perturb it by hand:
    • Physically Roll Right Wing Up: Right aileron must rise (acting to push the wing down); left aileron must drop.
    • Physically Pitch Nose Down: Both V-tail surfaces must deflect upward (acting to pull the nose back up).
    • Physically Pitch Nose Up: Both V-tail surfaces must deflect downward (acting to push the nose back down).
    ⚠️
    Stabilization Reversal Check: The control surfaces must actively oppose your hand motion to return the model to level. If the surfaces deflect in the direction of your movement, the aircraft will enter an uncontrollable dive or roll on launch.

Phase 5: Parameter Tweaks & Flight Readiness

  1. AHRS Level Cruise Pitch Calibration: Place the airframe on the workbench. Prop the nose up with a block so the fuselage sits at approximately $+3^\circ\text{ to }+5^\circ$ nose-up attitude. Navigate to: Setup -> Mandatory Hardware -> Accel Calibration. Click Calibrate Level.
  2. Resolve Pre-Arm Airspeed Fault: Attempting to arm without a pitot tube triggers PreArm: Check Airspeed. Go to: Config -> Full Parameter List. Search for ARSPD_TYPE and set it to 0 (Disabled). Search for ARSPD_ENABLE and set it to 0. Click Write Params.
  3. Enable Background Auto-Trim: In Full Parameter List, search for SERVO_AUTO_TRIM. Change value from 0 to 1 (Enabled). Click Write Params.
  4. Assign Maiden Flight Modes: In Initial Setup -> Mandatory Hardware -> Flight Modes, configure Channel 8:
    • Position 1: MANUAL (Emergency override)
    • Position 2: FBWA (Primary launch & cruising mode)
    • Position 3: CIRCLE (GPS loiter orbit check)
  5. Center of Gravity (CG) Physical Verification: Install the flight battery into its final strapped position. Balance the aircraft underneath the wings on the manufacturer's molded CG bumps. Ensure the airframe balances level or slightly nose-down (never tail-heavy!).

📚 Technical Terminology

Term Technical Definition Context & Practical Importance
Angle of Attack (AoA) The acute angle between the wing's chord line and the oncoming relative wind vector ($\alpha$). Governs lift generation; why calibrating AHRS level with $+3^\circ$ to $+5^\circ$ nose-up bias is essential for straight-and-level cruise.
FBWA (Fly-By-Wire A) An ArduPlane flight mode offering gyro-stabilized self-leveling with bank and pitch angle limits. The primary mode used for hand launches, cruise evaluation, and checking autopilot self-correction authority.
V-Tail Mixing An aerodynamic arrangement where two angled ruddervators combine pitch and yaw control. Calculated dynamically inside ArduPlane (VtailLeft and VtailRight) without requiring radio-side mixing.
BEC (Battery Eliminator Circuit) A DC-DC regulator integrated into the ESC stepping down high battery voltage to $+5.0\text{V}$. Powers the isolated servo positive rail on the Pixhawk via the Output 8 throttle cable lead.
SERVO_AUTO_TRIM An ArduPlane parameter enabling automated background subtrim tuning in flight. Records integrator effort in level cruise and writes offsets to SERVOx_TRIM, ensuring hands-off manual flight.
Center of Gravity (CG) The single theoretical balance point along the longitudinal axis where total aircraft weight concentrates. Autopilots cannot compensate for an excessively tail-heavy aircraft; must balance level or slightly nose-down on molded wing bumps.

💡 Practical Takeaways

  • 🔴 Never Reverse Channels in the Radio: Reverse backwards surfaces exclusively in Mission Planner's Servo Output menu. Reversing in the radio causes autopilot stabilization loops to fight pilot stick commands, causing a crash.
  • 🔴 Absolute Workshop Propeller Safety: Never connect a flight battery to configure servos or troubleshoot arming with a propeller installed on the motor shaft.
  • 🟢 Cut a Dedicated USB Service Port: Slice a small access window in the foam fuselage side aligned with the micro-USB connector. You will connect to Mission Planner repeatedly during flight commissioning.
  • 🟡 Plug Power Module into PM1: On dual-power carrier boards, always connect the Power Module harness to POWER 1 (PM1) to ensure the HUD reads active battery voltage and current.
  • ⚠️ The Two-Stage Surface Verification is Mandatory: Always verify that sticks deflect surfaces correctly in MANUAL, and that tilting the fuselage in FBWA forces surfaces to fight your motion.
  • 🟢 Calibrate Level with a Nose-Up Bias: Prop the nose up $+3^\circ$ to $+5^\circ$ when clicking Calibrate Level so the aircraft maintains altitude in autonomous cruise.
  • 🟢 Enable Auto-Trim: Set SERVO_AUTO_TRIM = 1 before maiden flight so the autopilot automatically trims the aircraft for level flight.

📝 Quick Revision (High-Yield Facts)

  • Forward Arrow Alignment: The arrow on the top of the Pixhawk Cube and the external GPS puck must point forward toward the aircraft nose.
  • Isolated Servo Rail: USB powers the CPU, sensors, and receiver; the servo rail center positive pin (+5V) is completely dead until energized by the ESC BEC.
  • Servo Output Routing: Typical Nano Talon routing: Output 1 = Aileron (4), Output 2 = VtailLeft (79), Output 3 = VtailRight (80), Output 8 = Throttle (70).
  • Airspeed Error Bypass: Disable missing pitot tube pre-arm errors by setting ARSPD_TYPE = 0 and ARSPD_ENABLE = 0.
  • Emergency Override Mode: Always assign MANUAL to Position 1 of your flight mode switch as an instant bailout if stabilization gains induce oscillations.
  • Center of Gravity: Always check physical balance on wing CG bumps with the flight battery strapped in its operational position.

❓ Questions & Answers (Self-Assessment)

Q1: Why must control surface reversing be configured in Mission Planner rather than on the radio transmitter?
If you reverse a channel on your transmitter, you alter only the human command signal sent to ArduPlane. The autopilot's internal stabilization algorithm (which senses physical vehicle rotation via gyroscopes) still pushes the control surface in the old direction. In stabilized modes (FBWA), the autopilot will deflect the surface backwards, amplify uncommanded vehicle motion, and cause an immediate crash. Reversing inside Mission Planner's Servo Output menu inverts the actual pin output, synchronizing both pilot commands and autopilot corrections.
Q2: Why do connected servos remain completely motionless when the Pixhawk is powered via USB?
The Pixhawk features physical electrical power isolation between its internal logic bus and the servo rail header. USB power energizes only the STM32 MCU, internal IMUs, and the RC IN receiver pins. The center positive pin (+5V) along MAIN OUT 1–8 receives zero voltage from USB to protect sensitive processor circuitry from servo back-EMF spikes and brownouts. An external 5V BEC (from the ESC throttle wire on Output 8) must be connected to power the servos.
Q3: How do you verify that autopilot stabilization is correcting in the proper direction in FBWA mode?
Switch the aircraft into FBWA mode and physically perturb the airframe by hand. Control surfaces must actively fight your motion to restore level flight: lifting the right wing must raise the right aileron (pushing the wing back down), and pitching the nose down must deflect elevators/ruddervators upward (pulling the nose back up).
Q4: Why should the artificial horizon level calibration be performed with the aircraft nose propped up +3° to +5°?
Fixed-wing aerofoils require a positive Angle of Attack (AoA) relative to oncoming airflow to generate aerodynamic lift. If an aircraft is calibrated dead flat on a table, the autopilot will hold the fuselage perfectly level in autonomous cruise, generating insufficient lift and causing the plane to steadily lose altitude unless manual elevator pressure is held.
Q5: What does the parameter SERVO_AUTO_TRIM = 1 accomplish during flight?
It instructs ArduPlane to sample the integrator effort of its PID stabilization loops during straight-and-level FBWA cruise. Every 10 seconds, it writes those trim offsets into the servo neutral registers (SERVOx_TRIM). When the pilot switches back to MANUAL mode, the aircraft remains trimmed for hands-off level flight without manual trim tab adjustments.
